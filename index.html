Why this topic matters

HIV in Kyrgyzstan is not an abstract issue. It is a real public health problem, and it is also a science communication problem. When people do not get clear and honest information, fear fills the gap. And when fear fills the gap, stigma grows.


According to official Kyrgyz data, 15,183 people are registered with HIV, and the majority of new infections are linked to sexual transmission and, in some cases, injections (Committee on HIV and TB KSOS at the Cabinet of Ministers of the Kyrgyz Republic, 2025). At the same time, the state provides free testing and free antiretroviral therapy (ART) for those who need it. This means the problem is not only medical. It is also about whether people know where to go, what to do, and whether they feel safe enough to ask for help.


The most painful part is that HIV is still surrounded by silence. In many families, schools, and public spaces, sex is still difficult to discuss openly. Because of that, many young people grow up hearing simple slogans like “Stop HIV!” but never learn how HIV is actually transmitted or prevented. They hear warnings, but not real explanations. They see posters, but not practical advice. As a result, myths survive, and people continue to fear what they do not understand.

Why silence makes the problem worse

In Kyrgyzstan, talking openly about sex is still uncomfortable for many people. This is true not only for ordinary citizens, but also for some teachers, journalists, and even public institutions. Because the topic is considered shameful, the most important part of HIV prevention is often left unspoken.


This silence has consequences. People living with HIV may be judged, avoided, or treated as if they are dangerous. Stigma is not just a feeling. It is a social reaction that makes people hide, delay testing, and avoid speaking honestly about their health (Herek, 1999). It also creates fear of being labeled as someone with an “improper” lifestyle. In the end, the illness becomes hidden, and the person becomes isolated.


We can already see how weak public communication has been in school campaigns. In school posters, students often write things like “Stop HIV/AIDS,” “Healthy Life,” or “Life Without Drugs”. These messages are not wrong, but they are incomplete. They do not explain the main way HIV is spread. They do not mention safe sex. They do not tell young people how to protect themselves. So the result is simple: people learn the slogans, but not the science.

What science says

HIV is not spread in random ways. It is transmitted through specific routes only. That is one of the most important things people need to understand.


This matters because a lot of fear comes from confusion. When people think HIV can spread through casual contact, they start avoiding people unnecessarily. That is where stigma becomes stronger than science. But once people know the real routes of transmission, the fear becomes smaller and the understanding becomes stronger.


This knowledge should not only protect people from infection. It should also protect people living with HIV from discrimination. Someone with HIV is not a danger in everyday life. They are not a threat just because they exist. They deserve support, not avoidance.

Why treatment changes everything

One of the most important messages in this article is hope.


HIV is not the end. With modern treatment, HIV can be managed. Early and regular ART can control the virus and help people live full lives (World Health Organization, 2025). This is why the phrase “HIV/AIDS – not the end!” is not just a slogan. It is a real message of hope.


This is also important for those who think diagnosis automatically means the end of normal life. It does not. People living with HIV can work, study, build relationships, raise children, and contribute to society. They can live a full life, especially when they receive treatment on time and follow medical advice.


There is also another important fact: treatment helps protect other people too. When HIV is properly controlled, the risk of transmission becomes much lower. This is one of the strongest reasons why testing and treatment matter so much. Knowing your status is not shameful. It is responsible.


That is why our campaign always gives a support route. We include the contact information and LINK for the Republican Center for AIDS Prevention and Control (RCAPC). We want the message to be simple: asking for help is not weakness. It is respect for yourself and protection for the people you love.

What people need to know

Testing, protection, and support matter.


First, people need to know their status. Testing is the first step. It gives clarity, and it gives a person the chance to act early.


Second, people need to understand prevention. In the current situation in Kyrgyzstan, the most effective way to protect yourself and others from HIV is the use of a condom during sex. This is not about fear or shame. It is about basic protection and responsibility.


Third, people need to stop confusing myths with facts. HIV is not spread through casual touch, shared meals, or daily contact. It spreads through specific routes only. Knowing this does two things at once: it helps people protect themselves, and it helps them stop discriminating against others.


Fourth, people need to hear real voices. This is very important. When HIV is discussed only through dry facts, it can feel distant. But when a real person speaks about living with HIV, the issue becomes human. It becomes real. That is why stories from people living with HIV matter so much. They do not just inform. They change the way others see the disease.


In Kyrgyzstan, and in the wider post-Soviet context, silence has been one of the biggest barriers. But silence can be broken. When young people hear the truth, when families talk more openly, when the media communicates carefully, and when experts speak in a way that ordinary people can understand, stigma starts to lose its power.

Final message

HIV is a medical condition, not a moral judgment. The culture of silence must end.


Every Kyrgyz citizen should know that HIV can be managed, that treatment matters, and that support makes a difference. People living with HIV do not deserve shame. They deserve respect, information, and care.


That is the message behind this project. Not fear. Not silence. But awareness, dignity, and action.

Myths vs facts

It is crucial to separate myths from reality. HIV is transmitted only in specific ways. The virus spreads through bodily fluids of an infected person:

Transmission routes

RouteWhat it means
SexUnprotected sexual contact.
BloodShared needles and non-sterile tattoo or piercing equipment.
Mother to childDuring childbirth or through breastfeeding.

Not transmitted through

MythReality
Everyday contactDishes, towels, toilets, swimming pools.
